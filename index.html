CPU Scheduling Algorithms — Laboratory Workbook

Structured lab record with formal Aim, Algorithm-Specific Pseudocode, C Programs, Sample I/O, and Viva Questions

1. Objective / Aim

To implement, simulate, and compare CPU Scheduling policies—FCFS, SJF / SRTF, Round Robin (RR), and Multi-Level Feedback Queue (MLFQ)—by constructing execution Gantt charts and evaluating Completion Time (CT), Turnaround Time (TAT), Waiting Time (WT), CPU Utilization, and Throughput.

2. Performance Metrics & Equations

Turnaround Time (TAT)
TAT = Completion Time (CT) − Arrival Time (AT)
Waiting Time (WT)
WT = Turnaround Time (TAT) − Burst Time (BT)
CPU Utilization (%)
(Total Busy Time / Total Elapsed Time) × 100
System Throughput
Completed Processes (N) / Total Elapsed Time

3. Policy Comparison Matrix

FCFS (Non-Preemptive)

Dispatches processes strictly in arrival order (FIFO). Simple, zero starvation, but vulnerable to the Convoy Effect when long CPU bursts arrive early.

SJF & SRTF

Prioritizes the shortest remaining CPU burst. Provably minimizes average waiting time, though long processes may suffer starvation.

Round Robin (RR)

Preemptive circular queue assigning each ready task a fixed Time Quantum (Q). Ideal for interactive time-sharing systems.

MLFQ (3-Level Feedback)

Separates interactive and CPU-bound tasks across priority queues (Q0 → Q1 → Q2) with Aging to prevent starvation.

Frequently Asked Lab Viva Questions

            

              Q1. What is the Convoy Effect in FCFS scheduling and how can it be observed?
              

                When one long CPU-bound process occupies the CPU first, several short interactive or I/O-bound processes queue up behind it, drastically increasing Average Waiting Time. Click Convoy Effect Demo in the top-left presets to see P1 (BT=14) delay P2, P3, and P4.
              

            

            

              Q2. Why is SJF/SRTF optimal for Average Waiting Time, and what is its main drawback?
              

                Moving a short job ahead of a long job reduces the waiting time of the short job more than it increases the waiting time of the long job, yielding the mathematical minimum average waiting time. Its primary drawback is starvation of long bursts when short jobs continually arrive, plus the difficulty of predicting exact future CPU burst lengths.
              

            

            

              Q3. How does Time Quantum (Q) selection impact Round Robin performance?
              

                If Q is very large, Round Robin behaves identically to FCFS. If Q is too small, excessive context switches degrade real-world CPU efficiency. Rule of thumb: choose Q so that ~80% of CPU bursts complete within a single quantum.
              

            

            

              Q4. How does MLFQ balance interactive responsiveness with starvation prevention?
              

                New jobs enter the highest-priority queue (Q0) with a short quantum so interactive jobs finish rapidly. CPU-bound jobs that exhaust their quantum are demoted to Q1 and Q2. To prevent indefinite starvation in lower queues, an Aging timer promotes long-waiting processes back to Q0.
